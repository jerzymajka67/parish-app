Fourth Sunday of Advent

Throughout Advent we have been reminded how God prepared His people, through the prophets, for the coming of His Son. We said that the last of the prophets of the Old Testament was John the Baptist — a man of fire, who preached repentance and conversion. He announced that the Kingdom of God was near: a kingdom of justice, where the Messiah would judge sinners.

And so, when Jesus — the Messiah — finally appears, John experiences disappointment. His expectations are not fulfilled. Jesus does not punish sinners; instead, He eats with them, while John himself suffers in Herod’s prison.

Today’s Gospel, however, shows us someone who could feel even more disappointed by the fulfillment of God’s plans — Saint Joseph.

We can imagine a young man preparing to marry his young fiancée. For Joseph, this marriage was probably the fulfillment of all his dreams. Soon he would stand before the altar to marry the most beautiful, the wisest, the most gentle and loving woman he could imagine.

And then, just when everything seems ready, Joseph learns that Mary is expecting a child — and that it is not his child.

This is a blow far greater than John’s disappointment. John’s struggle is religious and social, almost political. Joseph’s pain reaches the most intimate part of his life. He feels betrayed by the one he loves most — and that kind of pain cuts deepest.

Yet Joseph’s reaction is completely different. He does not shout, accuse, or demand explanations. He decides to withdraw quietly.

And then God speaks to him — not in a spectacular vision, not publicly as with Zechariah in the Temple, but in a dream. And the explanation Joseph receives is the most extraordinary of all: the child Mary carries is not the child of any man, but of God Himself.

Normally, when we have a strange dream, we wake up and say, “It was only a dream,” and quickly forget it. Joseph could have done the same. But he does not. He takes the dream seriously.

Why?

First, because Joseph loves Mary so deeply and trusts her so completely that it is easier for him to believe God is the Father of her child than to believe she has betrayed him. Second — and here we see the contrast with John the Baptist — Joseph has a heart especially sensitive to the Holy Spirit.

John is a man of spiritual battle. He stands firmly on the ground. He sees sin and its destructive power, in himself and in others. For him, God reveals Himself through judgment, power, and force.

Joseph is different. He recognizes God in silence. God speaks to him through gentleness and tenderness. Joseph is more inclined to believe in goodness than in evil. He trusts that God’s goodness is reflected especially in the human heart.

John cries out and prepares the way. Joseph says nothing — yet his quiet obedience changes history. John prepares for Jesus. Joseph receives Him into his home.

That is why Joseph is truly a man of the New Testament.

We need the courage and realism of John the Baptist. But our struggle must never harden our hearts. We must also learn from Joseph — to trust goodness more than evil, to listen for God not only in power and judgment, but in silence and love.

We need the warrior John. But even more, we need the praying mystic — Saint Joseph.

Lord God, teach us the heart of Saint Joseph — a heart that is quiet, attentive, and obedient to Your Spirit. Help us to trust Your love more than our fears and to recognize Your presence not only in what is loud and powerful, but also in the silence of our hearts.

Amen.
