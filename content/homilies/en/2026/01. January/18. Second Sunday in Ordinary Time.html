Second Sunday in Ordinary Time

At the beginning of Ordinary Time in the liturgical year, we start with the baptism of Jesus in the Jordan, so that we, together with the apostles, can accompany Him on His journey. For Jesus, this is the path to the full revelation of His glory through His death and resurrection, and for us, it is a path of growing closer to knowing Jesus and strengthening our faith in Him. And it is precisely the word “knowing” that I would like to draw your special attention to today.

We heard a passage from the Gospel of John, written against false gnosis — against a false knowledge of Jesus. In the time when John, the last of the Apostles, wrote his Gospel, many Christians came from gnostic backgrounds — people of Greek culture who treated Christianity as a way to know God, but often understood this knowledge as a detachment from earthly matters, a turning inward into meditation and philosophy.

John, however, emphasizes that we can truly know God only through Christ — not through an imagined Christ, but through the one who really walked on earth, taught, ate with the apostles, and whose body they could touch. God reveals Himself not primarily in our thoughts or imagination, but in what we actually see, hear, and touch.

At the same time, John notes that the experience of Jesus — the Man — must be constantly processed through the effort of our faith. It is through this effort, knowing Jesus the Man, that we gradually come to know Jesus the God. In today’s Gospel, we see John the Baptist saying about Jesus: “I did not know Him before.” Is this not surprising? Did Jesus and John not know each other even in their mothers’ wombs? Although the Gospels do not tell us this, the oldest tradition tells us that they knew each other as children.

Why, then, does John say: “I did not know Him before”? Because up to that point, he knew Jesus only as his cousin. Only through the Holy Spirit does he come to know Him as the Savior. Now he sees Him in His full truth and bears witness that Jesus is the Son of God — the one we are to listen to and obey.

Our task, then, is to continually make the effort to know God anew in what we hear, in what we see, what we can touch, and even in what we receive in the sacraments. At the same time, this knowledge of Jesus should lead us to a deeper understanding that He truly came to save us — to save us from our sins.

For what makes us truly unhappy is not poverty, illness, or fear of death. It is sin, because sin separates us from God and makes us its slave. To know Jesus is to understand that His teaching is the only path to true happiness.
