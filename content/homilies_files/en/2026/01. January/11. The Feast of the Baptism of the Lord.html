The Feast of the Baptism of the Lord

As we conclude the Christmas season and begin Ordinary Time, we celebrate the Feast of the Baptism of the Lord. This event is the full revelation of Jesus as Savior, a revelation that began on the day of His birth. After His baptism in the Jordan, Jesus is no longer only Jesus, the son of the carpenter from Nazareth, but Jesus Christ, the Savior of the world.

Today’s feast therefore reminds us of Jesus’ baptism in the Jordan, but at the same time it directs our attention to our own baptism. Our baptism shares certain elements with the baptism of John, but it is something far greater.

John’s baptism was a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins. In this way, the chosen people prepared themselves for the coming of the Messiah by washing away the stain of sin. We too, in baptism, are washed clean from sin — but in order to receive the Holy Spirit, the very same Spirit who descends upon Jesus in the Jordan.

This gift is absolutely necessary if we are to recognize and confess Jesus as the Christ, the Savior of the world. Without the Holy Spirit, we often become disappointed with Jesus’ mission. We expected that with His coming — just as the prophets of the Old Testament foretold, including Isaiah whom we heard today — the fate of the world would be completely transformed. And yet, it often seems that nothing has really changed.

Isaiah, in his prophecy, repeats the word “justice” three times: justice for the nations, justice on the earth, the victory of justice. And so we may ask: where is this justice? Is it not true that today, just as in the time of Jesus, this word is used constantly? If the coming of the Messiah brought the victory of justice, why do we still have to fight for it?

But what kind of justice is Isaiah speaking about? And what kind of justice do we usually have in mind? Is it not often the case that justice, for us, means the victory of what we believe is good for ourselves — for our group, our family, our nation, or our political party? And very often we call upon God to fight on our side for such a version of justice.

In the year 1410, one of the bloodiest battles of medieval Europe took place: the Battle of Grunwald. On one side fought Polish knights; on the other, the knights of the Teutonic Order of the Blessed Virgin Mary, known as the Teutonic Knights, mostly Germans. Both sides sang hymns in honor of the Blessed Virgin Mary before the battle — one in Polish, the other in German. Both sides invoked the help of Mary and of her Son, Jesus Christ, in their fight for justice. This is what human justice looks like.

But what kind of justice did Jesus bring? What is God’s justice?

We heard about it in the second reading, when Peter says that “God shows no partiality,” but desires the salvation of all — regardless of nationality, race, gender, or social status. This is precisely what divine justice means: for God, we are all His children. It does not matter whom you kill — you kill a child of God. It does not matter whom you hate — you hate someone whom God loves and for whom His Son died on the cross.

Yet in order to understand this divine justice and truly accept it in our hearts, baptism is necessary: the washing away of sin and the gift of the Holy Spirit. Mere cleansing from sin is not enough; striving for self-perfection, as the Pharisees did, is not enough. We also need the light of the Holy Spirit. But even receiving the Spirit in baptism is not sufficient if we allow sin to silence God’s voice within us.

Almost all the knights at Grunwald were baptized, and yet they killed one another with the names of Jesus and Mary on their lips.

Today, as we remember the baptism of Jesus in the Jordan, let us pray that we may truly be open to the gifts God has given us in baptism: the strength to fight against sin so that it does not rule over us, and the light of the Holy Spirit, which enables us to recognize Jesus, the Son of Mary, as the Christ and Savior — the One who brought renewal to the world and who still needs us, so that this renewal may become ever more visible.
