Second Sunday of Lent

On the First Sunday of Lent, we spoke about sin — about how sin is connected with the temptation of Satan, who shows us a “shortcut.” He promises us Easter Sunday without the necessity of passing through Good Friday.

On the Second Sunday of Lent, the biblical readings present another key concept of this season — conversion. “Repent and believe in the Gospel.” But conversion is shown here in a deeper perspective. It is no longer only a struggle against sin; it is becoming a new person.

What does this transformation mean?

In the first reading, we hear about the call of Abraham. God makes great promises to him. Yet the condition for their fulfillment is the complete leaving behind of his old life — abandoning everything he possessed in order to set out toward something new, toward the unknown.

This is the calling of every Christian.

On the day of our baptism, each of us was called to be clothed in a new garment, to change our life. We are meant to live not only for what this earthly reality can offer us, but above all to strive for the New Promised Land that God has promised by sending His Son.

Jesus speaks of this same transformation when He tells His disciples that no one can serve two masters. One cannot remain in Ur and at the same time set out for the Promised Land. To begin the journey toward the new, we must leave behind the old. We must let go of what is familiar in order to reach what is unknown.

Yet the disciples need at least some vision of this “new” that Jesus proposes. That is why, preparing them for the scandal of the Cross, Jesus takes them up the mountain of the Transfiguration to show them at least a glimpse of the glory that will surround Him after the Resurrection.

It might seem that Jesus’ intention did not succeed. Peter, overwhelmed by the glory on the mountain, says, “Lord, it is good that we are here,” and yet on Good Friday he denies Him three times. He forgets what he saw. He forgets the words of Jesus. On that night from Thursday to Friday, he sees only a weak and humiliated Jesus.

And yet, it is this same Peter who ultimately gives his life for his faith.

Peter’s example shows us that the journey of faith is not based only on a single powerful experience — even the most moving retreat, inspiration, or vision. And yet such experiences are necessary. To pass through Good Friday, we must keep before our eyes the perspective of Easter Sunday.

We must be prepared for many “Fridays” in our lives. In moments of difficulty, doubts will come. That is why every year the Church invites us to relive the season of Lent — so that, together with the Apostles, we may allow Jesus to prepare us for the trials of faith and renew within us the desire to share in the glory of the Risen Lord.

So that — like Abraham — hearing God’s call to leave behind our old way of life, we may keep before us the vision of the Promised Land of eternal life.
